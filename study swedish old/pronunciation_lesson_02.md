# Pronunciation Lesson 2: Swedish Vowel Sounds — A, E, I, O, U, Y

## What You'll Learn
Swedish has 9 vowels (A, E, I, O, U, Y, Å, Ä, Ö) and each one has both a long and a short version. In this lesson you'll master the six "standard" vowels. You already know Å, Ä, and Ö from Lesson 1 — now let's complete the picture.

---

## The Golden Rule: Long vs Short Vowels

Before diving into each vowel, you need to understand the most important rule in Swedish pronunciation:

**Every Swedish vowel has two versions — long and short.**

How do you know which one to use? It depends on what comes after the vowel:

- **Long vowel:** When the vowel is followed by ONE consonant (or no consonant). The vowel stretches out.
- **Short vowel:** When the vowel is followed by TWO or more consonants. The vowel is clipped short.

**Example with A:**
- **gata** /GAH-tah/ — street (long A, because only one "t" follows)
- **katt** /kaht/ — cat (short A, because "tt" = two consonants follow)

This rule applies to ALL nine Swedish vowels. It's one of the reasons Swedish pronunciation is actually quite predictable once you know the system.

## The Six Standard Vowels

### A a

**Long A** — Like the "a" in "father." Open, relaxed, at the back of the mouth.
- **gata** /GAH-tah/ — street
- **tala** /TAH-lah/ — to speak
- **dag** /dahg/ — day
- **mat** /maht/ — food

**Short A** — Shorter and slightly more closed, but still an "ah" sound. Like a quick version of the long A.
- **katt** /kaht/ — cat
- **hatt** /haht/ — hat
- **vacker** /VAH-ker/ — beautiful
- **kall** /kahl/ — cold

**Common mistake:** English speakers sometimes use the "a" from "cat" (the flat, nasal American A). Swedish A is always rounder, more like "ah."

---

### E e

**Long E** — Like the "ay" in "say" but without the glide at the end. Keep it pure — don't let it slide into "ee."
- **leka** /LEH-kah/ — to play
- **veta** /VEH-tah/ — to know
- **se** /seh/ — to see
- **tre** /treh/ — three

**Short E** — Like the "e" in "met" or "pet." Quick and open.
- **gett** /yeht/ — given
- **penna** /PEH-nah/ — pen
- **semester** /seh-MES-ter/ — vacation
- **vecka** /VEH-kah/ — week

**Tricky detail:** Unstressed E at the end of words often reduces to a schwa sound (like the "a" in "sofa"). For example, "gata" sounds more like /GAH-tah/ where the final "a" is soft and lazy. Same applies to final E — "pojke" (boy) sounds like /POY-keh/ with a soft ending.

---

### I i

**Long I** — Like the "ee" in "see" or "feel." Bright and high.
- **tid** /teed/ — time
- **fika** /FEE-kah/ — fika (coffee break)
- **bil** /beel/ — car
- **vi** /vee/ — we

**Short I** — Like the "i" in "sit" or "bit." Quick and more relaxed than the long version.
- **till** /til/ — to, until
- **flicka** /FLIK-ah/ — girl
- **ditt** /dit/ — your (ett-words)
- **vill** /vil/ — want

**Common mistake:** Don't make long I into a diphthong (don't say "ee-yuh"). Keep it pure and steady.

---

### O o

**Long O** — This is where English speakers get surprised. Swedish long O usually sounds like "oo" in "food" — NOT like the English "oh."
- **bok** /book/ — book
- **sol** /sool/ — sun
- **bro** /broo/ — bridge
- **stol** /stool/ — chair
- **god** /good/ — good

**Short O** — Sounds like a short "o" as in British "hot" or the "u" in "put." More open and rounded.
- **bott** /boht/ — lived (past tense of bo)
- **kopp** /kop/ — cup
- **ost** /oost/ — cheese (this one leans toward long)
- **tropp** /trop/ — troop

**This is the big one.** English speakers consistently get Swedish O wrong. When you see an O in Swedish, your default should be an "oo" sound, not an "oh" sound. This single adjustment will dramatically improve how you sound.

**Important exceptions:** Before R, the O often sounds more like the English "or" — for example, "stor" /stoor/ (big) keeps the "oo," but "ord" /oord/ (word) gets close to it too. Just listen and adjust over time.

---

### U u

**Long U** — This sound doesn't exist in English. Round your lips very tightly (like you're about to whistle) and try to say "ee." The result is Swedish U.
- **hus** /hews/ — house
- **du** /dew/ — you
- **ful** /fewl/ — ugly
- **mur** /mewr/ — wall
- **ljus** /yews/ — light, candle

**Short U** — Similar but quicker and slightly more open.
- **buss** /buhs/ — bus
- **hund** /hoond/ — dog
- **full** /fuhl/ — full, drunk
- **under** /OON-der/ — under, wonder

**This is the hardest standard vowel for English speakers.** It's NOT the "oo" in "food" (that's closer to Swedish O). It's NOT the "u" in "but." It's a completely unique sound that requires tight, rounded lips. Practice by saying "ee" while slowly rounding your lips into a tight circle.

---

### Y y

**Long Y** — Another sound that doesn't exist in English. It's like Swedish U but even tighter. Round your lips extremely tightly and try to say "ee."
- **by** /byh/ — village
- **ny** /nyh/ — new
- **typ** /tyhp/ — type, kind of
- **lysa** /LYH-sah/ — to shine
- **syster** /SYH-ster/ — sister

**Short Y** — Quicker and slightly more open.
- **nytt** /nyht/ — new (ett-form)
- **mygg** /myhg/ — mosquito
- **trygg** /tryhg/ — safe, secure
- **bygga** /BYH-gah/ — to build

**Y is always a vowel in Swedish** — never a consonant like in English "yes." The "y" sound in English "yes" is represented by J in Swedish (you'll learn this in a later lesson).

**How Y differs from U:** Both require rounded lips, but Y is produced with the tongue higher and further forward in the mouth. Think of it this way: U is rounded with the tongue pulled back, Y is rounded with the tongue pushed forward. In practice, they sound very similar to untrained ears — and that's okay. Swedes will understand you either way.

## Long vs Short: Practice Pairs

These pairs show how the same vowel letter changes between long and short. Say them out loud:

| Long Vowel | Meaning | Short Vowel | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|------------|---------|
| **glas** /glahs/ | glass | **glass** /glahs/ | ice cream |
| **tak** /tahk/ | roof | **tack** /tahk/ | thanks |
| **vin** /veen/ | wine | **vinn** /vin/ | win! |
| **fil** /feel/ | file, lane | **fill** /fil/ | fleece |
| **bok** /book/ | book | **bock** /bohk/ | buck, ram |
| **hus** /hews/ | house | **russ** /ruhs/ | small horse |
| **ny** /nyh/ | new | **nytt** /nyht/ | new (ett-form) |

**Notice the pattern:** Double consonants = short vowel. Single consonant = long vowel. This is incredibly consistent in Swedish.

## The Vowel Groups

Swedish divides its vowels into two groups that affect how consonants are pronounced (this becomes crucial in later lessons):

**Hard vowels (back vowels):** A, O, U, Å
**Soft vowels (front vowels):** E, I, Y, Ä, Ö

Why does this matter? Because the consonants K, G, and SK change their pronunciation depending on which group of vowel follows them. You'll learn this in detail in Lessons 3 and 4 — but start noticing the groups now.

## Summary

Swedish has 9 vowels, each with a long and short version — that's 18 vowel sounds total. The system is actually very logical once you understand the rule:

**What you learned:**
- Every vowel has a long version (before single consonant) and short version (before double consonant)
- A = "ah" (never the flat English "a" from "cat")
- E = "eh" to "ay" (pure, no glide)
- I = "ee" (bright and clean)
- O = usually "oo" — NOT the English "oh" (the biggest surprise)
- U = round your lips tightly and say "ee" (unique to Swedish)
- Y = like U but tighter and more forward (always a vowel, never a consonant)
- Vowels split into hard (A, O, U, Å) and soft (E, I, Y, Ä, Ö) groups

**The three biggest takeaways:**
1. Swedish O sounds like "oo" — this changes everything
2. Swedish U is a completely new sound — practice the lip rounding
3. Double consonants = short vowel, single consonant = long vowel
